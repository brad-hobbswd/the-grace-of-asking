Chapter One

Why Are We Always Sorry?

Why Are We Always Sorry?

There is something interesting about the way we communicate with the people we love. Sometimes, before we even ask for something, we have already decided that our request might be too much. We approach a conversation with an apology, soften our words, explain why we are asking, and sometimes even convince ourselves that we should not have asked at all. We say, “I’m sorry to bother you,” when all we wanted was a little help. We say, “I’m sorry for asking,” when the person we are asking has given us no indication that our request is unwelcome. Without realizing it, we begin treating ordinary communication as though it requires permission to exist.

I have found myself thinking about this in the context of relationships, particularly when it comes to asking a partner for something. Imagine asking someone you love to help you with something simple, something they are capable of doing, and something that is not an unreasonable request. Before they can even respond, the words come out: “I’m sorry for asking.” But why are we apologizing? If the other person is willing to help, why do we feel the need to apologize for giving them the opportunity? And if we are the person being asked, why would we want someone we love to feel that asking us is an inconvenience?

Sometimes the person receiving the request is not upset at all. They are not frustrated, they are not feeling pressured, and they may genuinely be happy to help. Yet the person asking continues to feel uncomfortable. This raises a question worth considering: Are we responding to the person in front of us, or are we responding to something we have learned to believe about ourselves?

Perhaps we have become so accustomed to handling everything independently that accepting help feels unfamiliar. Perhaps previous experiences have taught us that asking comes with frustration, disappointment, or criticism. Maybe we have asked someone for help in the past and been made to feel that we were asking too much. Over time, those experiences can influence how we approach even the healthiest relationships. We begin apologizing before anyone has expressed frustration because we have already anticipated a negative response.

But we must be careful not to allow yesterday’s experiences to determine how we receive today’s kindness.

There is nothing wrong with being independent. In fact, personal responsibility is an important part of maturity. We should recognize what we are capable of doing for ourselves rather than expecting someone else to take responsibility for everything. However, independence should never become a reason to reject every opportunity to receive help. Being capable of doing something yourself does not mean you must always do everything alone. Relationships are not competitions in which each person must prove how little they need the other.

Think about how you respond when someone you love asks you for help. Do you immediately consider whether you have the time and ability to help them, or do you make them feel guilty for asking? Now consider the opposite situation. When someone offers to help you, do you accept their kindness, or do you immediately begin apologizing because you feel as though you have inconvenienced them? Sometimes we are much more comfortable being the person who gives than the person who receives. Giving allows us to feel useful, capable, and in control, while receiving requires us to acknowledge that there are moments when we need someone else.

That realization can be uncomfortable, especially for people who have spent much of their lives trying not to depend on anyone.

However, there is a difference between depending on someone for everything and allowing someone to be part of your life. A relationship should not require either person to become completely self-sufficient to prove their worth. There should be room for one person to help the other without keeping score, just as there should be room for someone to decline a request without being made to feel guilty. Love does not eliminate personal boundaries, but neither should boundaries become an excuse to withhold kindness.

Proverbs 17:17 reminds us that a friend loves at all times. That kind of love speaks to consistency, care, and the willingness to remain present through the ordinary moments of life. Sometimes those moments are not dramatic or life-changing. They are found in the small requests, the unexpected needs, and the simple opportunities to show consideration. How we respond to those moments can reveal much about the way we understand love.

We should also examine the apologies we offer ourselves. How often do we feel guilty for needing rest, asking a question, requesting assistance, or admitting that something is difficult? How often do we expect ourselves to handle everything without ever needing anyone? We can become so focused on not being a burden that we forget relationships are meant to involve mutual care. We may never intend to push people away, but constantly refusing to receive can create distance just as surely as constantly demanding can.

The answer is not to stop being considerate. It is to understand when an apology is appropriate and when it is unnecessary. If we have interrupted someone’s important responsibility, disregarded their time, or placed an unreasonable expectation on them, an apology may be appropriate. But if we have simply asked someone we love for reasonable help, we do not need to begin the conversation by condemning ourselves for asking. We can ask respectfully, allow them to respond honestly, and accept their answer without turning the moment into something it was never meant to be.

Perhaps the next time we find ourselves saying, “I’m sorry for asking,” we should pause and consider why those words came so naturally. Did we actually do something wrong, or are we simply uncomfortable with the possibility of needing someone? Are we being considerate, or have we convinced ourselves that our needs are an inconvenience to everyone around us?

Grace begins when we learn to recognize the difference.

We cannot expect others to receive our kindness freely while we continually reject theirs. We cannot encourage people to be gentle with themselves while holding ourselves to an impossible standard. And we cannot build healthy relationships if every request is accompanied by guilt and every act of assistance feels like an obligation.

The next time someone you love asks you for something, consider the grace you can extend by allowing them to ask without shame. And the next time you need something, remember that asking does not automatically make you a burden. You are responsible for your actions, considerate of another person’s circumstances, and respectful of their answer, but you do not have to apologize simply because you allowed someone an opportunity to help.
