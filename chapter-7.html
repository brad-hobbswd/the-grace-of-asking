Chapter Seven

Stop Apologizing for Being Human

Stop Apologizing for Being Human

There comes a point when we need to examine the words we use so frequently that we no longer recognize what they communicate about how we see ourselves. An apology is important when we have hurt someone, acted carelessly, broken trust, or failed to take responsibility for our actions. But what happens when apologizing becomes our automatic response to simply having needs, expressing feelings, asking questions, or accepting help? At what point does being considerate turn into believing that we must apologize for taking up space in someone else’s life?

We can become so accustomed to saying sorry that we begin using it in situations where it does not belong. We apologize for being tired, for needing a moment, for asking someone to help us, or for wanting a little attention from the person we love. We apologize when we speak up, when we need reassurance, and sometimes even when we are having a difficult day. Rather than recognizing these things as ordinary parts of being human, we treat them as inconveniences that require an explanation.

I think many of us have experienced moments when we wanted to ask someone for something but hesitated because we did not want to bother them. We considered whether the request was worth making, whether the person would become frustrated, and whether we should simply take care of it ourselves. Sometimes we eventually ask, but the request comes with an apology that seems almost automatic. We say we are sorry even when the person has shown no irritation and has willingly offered to help.

What makes this especially interesting is that we often do not treat other people the same way. When someone we love asks us for assistance, we may gladly help without thinking twice. We do not consider their request an inconvenience, and we do not believe they need to apologize for approaching us. Yet when we find ourselves in their position, we struggle to receive the same kindness. We extend understanding outward while withholding it inward.

Perhaps we need to recognize that constantly apologizing does not necessarily make us more humble, considerate, or loving. Sometimes it reveals that we have become uncomfortable with our own needs. We may believe that the less we ask of others, the easier we are to love. We may convince ourselves that being independent means never needing assistance, or that being a good partner means never creating an inconvenience. But those expectations can become exhausting because they leave very little room for the ordinary realities of life.

Ecclesiastes 3:1 reminds us that there is a season and a time for every matter or purpose under heaven. Life does not remain in one condition. There are moments when we are strong and moments when we are tired, moments when we can give and moments when we need to receive. We will not always have the same abilities, the same energy, or the same circumstances. Recognizing that reality is not weakness. It is part of understanding the life God has given us.

We must also be careful not to confuse humility with making ourselves insignificant. Humility does not require us to believe that our needs do not matter. It does not mean refusing kindness, silencing our feelings, or apologizing every time we express what is happening inside us. We can recognize that other people have responsibilities without deciding that our own concerns are unimportant.

There is a difference between saying, “I’m sorry I interrupted you,” and saying, “I’m sorry for asking you to help me.” The first acknowledges something that may have affected another person. The second may be an unnecessary apology for a reasonable request. Understanding that distinction helps us communicate more honestly and prevents us from carrying guilt that does not belong to us.

This does not mean we should stop being thoughtful. We should still consider another person’s time, respect their boundaries, and acknowledge when our actions have caused harm. We should still apologize when an apology is genuinely needed. But we should not turn every interaction into an opportunity to criticize ourselves. There is no virtue in constantly making ourselves feel guilty for being part of someone else’s life.

Think about what might happen if we began responding differently. Instead of saying, “I’m sorry to bother you,” we could say, “Do you have a moment to help me?” Instead of apologizing for asking, we could simply express what we need and allow the other person to answer. Instead of feeling guilty when someone willingly does something kind, we could say, “Thank you. I appreciate you.” These changes may seem small, but they reflect a healthier understanding of communication and relationships.

Gratitude is not the same as guilt. We can appreciate someone’s effort without believing that we have done something wrong by receiving it. We can acknowledge their kindness without feeling that we must immediately repay it. We can be thankful that someone is willing to help while recognizing that healthy relationships involve moments of giving and receiving.

And perhaps that is where we need to begin: by allowing ourselves to be human without constantly treating our humanity as a problem. We will have needs. We will have questions. We will have moments when we need assistance, reassurance, patience, or understanding. Those things do not automatically make us difficult to love.

God does not call us to live under constant condemnation, and we should not create that environment within our relationships or within our own hearts. We can take responsibility when we are wrong, extend grace when others struggle, and receive kindness when it is offered. We do not have to apologize for every moment in which we need someone else.

The next time you feel the words “I’m sorry” coming out of your mouth, pause for a moment. Ask yourself whether you have actually done something that requires an apology or whether you are simply uncomfortable with having a need. If you have hurt someone, take responsibility. If you have made a mistake, acknowledge it. But if you have simply asked someone you love for reasonable help, allow yourself to receive their answer without unnecessary guilt.

You are allowed to have a voice, express a need, ask a question, and receive kindness. You can be considerate of others without constantly diminishing yourself. And you can walk in humility without believing that your presence is an inconvenience.
