Chapter Four

Love Should Make Room for Asking

Love Should Make Room for Asking

One of the things we should be able to experience in a healthy relationship is the freedom to communicate without constantly wondering whether we are asking too much. That does not mean every request must be granted, every need must be met immediately, or every inconvenience must be ignored. It means there should be enough trust between two people that asking for something does not automatically become an uncomfortable experience. Love should make room for honest conversations, reasonable requests, and the understanding that neither person will always have everything figured out.

Think about the difference between asking someone who makes you feel welcome and asking someone who makes you feel like an inconvenience. The request may be exactly the same, but the experience can be completely different. With one person, you can speak freely because you trust that they will listen. With another, you may find yourself carefully choosing your words, explaining why you need something, and apologizing before you have even finished your sentence. Over time, those differences can influence how comfortable we feel communicating within our relationships.

Sometimes we do not realize how much a simple response can matter. When someone asks us for help, we have an opportunity to respond with kindness, even when we are unable to meet the request. A simple, “I can’t do that right now, but perhaps I can help later,” communicates something very different from an irritated response that makes the person regret asking. We may not always have the time, energy, or ability to help, but we can still treat one another with respect.

I think this is especially important between partners because relationships involve everyday moments that rarely receive much attention. We often think about love in terms of major sacrifices, important milestones, and the promises we make to one another. Yet much of the experience of loving someone is found in ordinary interactions. It is found in how we answer a question, how we respond when someone needs assistance, and whether we make room for the other person to express themselves without feeling foolish for doing so.

There may be times when your partner asks you to do something that they could easily accomplish themselves. Perhaps it would simply be easier if you helped, or perhaps they enjoy having you involved. You may be perfectly willing to do it, and the request may not create any difficulty for you. Yet they respond by apologizing for asking. Rather than becoming frustrated with them, it may be worth reassuring them that asking is not a problem. You can remind them that you are willing to help while also maintaining the freedom to communicate when you cannot.

However, reassurance must be accompanied by consistency. If we tell someone they can ask us for help but repeatedly respond with irritation, resentment, or reminders of what we have done for them, our actions eventually contradict our words. People learn whether it is safe to approach us not simply by what we promise but by how we respond when they actually need something.

Ephesians 4:32 calls us to be kind and compassionate toward one another, forgiving one another as God in Christ forgave us. Kindness is not limited to the moments when everything is convenient. It also matters when someone approaches us with a request we did not expect or when we need to communicate that we cannot help. Compassion does not require us to abandon our own needs, but it does challenge us to consider how our words and reactions affect the people we love.

At the same time, we must understand that creating room for someone to ask does not mean creating an expectation that we must always say yes. Love should never become an arrangement in which one person feels obligated to fulfill every request simply to prove their commitment. Both people have responsibilities, limitations, and personal needs. A healthy relationship allows each person to express those realities without using them to punish the other.

There is a difference between saying no because you genuinely cannot help and saying no in a way that makes someone feel ashamed for asking. There is also a difference between asking because you would appreciate assistance and demanding that someone do something simply because they are your partner. Understanding those differences allows us to communicate with greater maturity and consideration.

Perhaps we should ask ourselves what kind of environment we are creating in our relationships. Do the people closest to us feel comfortable telling us what they need? Can they approach us without preparing for an argument? Can we receive a request without immediately assuming that someone is taking advantage of us? And when we cannot help, can we communicate that without making the other person feel that they should never have asked?

Love should not require people to become afraid of communicating. It should provide room for honesty, consideration, and understanding. Sometimes the greatest reassurance we can offer someone is not a promise that we will always do everything they ask, but the confidence that they can come to us without being made to feel guilty for asking.

We should also remember that the freedom to ask must be accompanied by the freedom to answer. When both people understand that, requests no longer have to become tests of love, and saying no does not automatically become a rejection. Instead, communication becomes an opportunity to understand one another more fully.

The people we love should not have to measure every word as though one simple request could become a problem. Neither should we feel that we must surrender our own boundaries to prove that we care. Love makes room for both people, and grace helps us remember that neither person has to be perfect in order to be treated with kindness.
