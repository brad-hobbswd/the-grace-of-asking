You Are Not a Burden

You Are Not a Burden

Perhaps by now you have begun to recognize some of your own experiences in these pages. Maybe you have been the person who apologizes before asking for help, or perhaps you have been the person wondering why someone you love feels the need to apologize for approaching you. Maybe you have struggled to receive kindness, or perhaps you have given so much of yourself that you have forgotten the importance of allowing others to carry some of the weight. Wherever you find yourself, I hope you have come to understand that grace is not something we should make difficult for one another to experience.

There is nothing wrong with asking someone for help when you need it, and there is nothing wrong with accepting assistance when it is willingly offered. We must remain responsible for our own actions and considerate of the people around us, but we should never confuse responsibility with the belief that we must do everything alone. Being capable does not mean we will never need help, and being independent does not mean we must refuse every opportunity to receive it.

I also hope we have learned to pay attention to the way we respond to the people who approach us. A simple request can become an opportunity to show kindness, patience, and understanding. We may not always be able to help, and we should never feel obligated to say yes when we cannot. But we can communicate our limitations without making someone feel ashamed for asking. Sometimes the greatest kindness is allowing someone to know that they can approach us honestly, even when the answer may not always be what they hoped for.

More importantly, we must learn to recognize when our apologies are appropriate and when they are simply expressions of unnecessary guilt. When we have hurt someone, we should take responsibility. When we have made a mistake, we should acknowledge it. When we have placed an unreasonable expectation on another person, we should be willing to reconsider our actions. But when we have simply asked a reasonable question, expressed a need, or accepted an act of kindness, we do not need to condemn ourselves for being human.

God never intended for us to live as though every need is a personal failure. His grace reminds us that we are not defined by our imperfections, and His mercy gives us the opportunity to grow without remaining imprisoned by yesterday’s mistakes. We can extend that same spirit of compassion to the people around us while learning to receive it ourselves.

So the next time you find yourself saying, “I’m sorry for asking,” pause and consider whether an apology is truly necessary. The next time someone asks you for help, remember that you have the freedom to answer honestly while still responding with kindness. And the next time someone willingly does something for you, allow yourself to say thank you without feeling that you must apologize for accepting their generosity.

Love should never become a place where people are afraid to express their needs, and grace should never become something we offer everyone except ourselves.

You are allowed to ask. You are allowed to receive. You are allowed to say no when you cannot help, and you are allowed to respect another person’s no without questioning their love. You can take responsibility for your life while still allowing others to walk alongside you.

Perhaps that is what we needed to understand all along: we were never meant to measure love by how little we ask of one another, but by how we treat one another through the ordinary moments of life.

You are not a burden simply because you need something. And someone who loves you should not have to feel guilty for allowing you to receive it.

Let grace go both ways.
