Chapter Six

The Freedom to Say Yes or No

The Freedom to Say Yes or No

One of the greatest misunderstandings in relationships is the belief that saying yes is always an expression of love and saying no is somehow a failure to show it. We can become so concerned about disappointing someone that we agree to things we do not have the time, energy, or ability to do. We may say yes because we feel obligated, because we do not want an argument, or because we believe refusing a request will make the other person question how much we care. But love should never require us to abandon honesty simply to keep someone else happy.

There is a difference between being willing to help and feeling that we have no choice but to help. When someone asks us for something, we should be able to consider the request honestly. Perhaps we have the time and would be happy to do it. Perhaps we are already occupied and need to explain that we cannot. Neither answer should automatically become a measure of how much we love the person asking.

Think about the partner who is asked to do something simple. They may be perfectly willing to help, and the request may not create any difficulty at all. Yet the person asking apologizes repeatedly, almost as though they have placed an unfair expectation on them. The partner may respond, “You don’t have to apologize. I’m happy to help.” That response is an opportunity to remind the other person that asking is allowed and that their request does not have to become a source of guilt.

But consider the opposite situation. What happens when the partner cannot help? Perhaps they are exhausted, occupied with another responsibility, or simply need time for themselves. If they say no, does the other person accept the answer, or do they interpret it as rejection? Do they become frustrated, withdraw emotionally, or make the partner feel guilty for not fulfilling the request?

Both situations deserve our attention because grace must exist on both sides of a conversation. The person asking should have the freedom to communicate without shame, and the person answering should have the freedom to respond honestly without fear of punishment.

Matthew 5:37 teaches the importance of letting our yes be yes and our no be no. Although this Scripture addresses truthful communication, its principle is important in our relationships as well. We should not say yes when we mean no simply because we are afraid of someone’s reaction. Neither should we use no as a way to punish someone for having asked. Honesty allows people to understand where they stand without having to interpret resentment, silence, or hidden expectations.

Sometimes we need to learn that a respectful no does not mean someone has stopped caring. It may simply mean that they cannot meet a particular request at that moment. We should not make someone prove their love by agreeing to everything we ask, just as we should not feel obligated to agree to everything someone asks of us. Love cannot flourish when every answer becomes a test of commitment.

This also requires us to examine how we respond when we do not get the answer we hoped for. It is easy to appreciate someone’s kindness when they say yes, but maturity is revealed when we can respect their answer even when it is no. We may feel disappointed, and we may need to find another solution, but disappointment does not give us permission to make someone feel guilty for having limitations.

Likewise, if we are the person saying no, we should communicate with consideration. We do not need to be harsh, dismissive, or defensive. We can explain our circumstances without making the other person regret approaching us. There is a difference between setting a boundary and making someone feel unwelcome. A boundary communicates what we can reasonably offer, while an unkind response can make another person feel ashamed for having a need.

I believe this is where many relationships can grow. We must become comfortable with honest communication rather than relying on assumptions. If we are willing to help, we can say so without making the other person feel indebted. If we cannot help, we can say so without creating unnecessary conflict. And if we are the person asking, we can accept either answer without allowing our self-worth to depend on the response.

There is also something freeing about realizing that we do not have to earn the right to ask by proving that we never need anything. We do not have to make every request perfectly convenient, nor do we have to anticipate every possible limitation before opening our mouths. We simply need to approach others with respect and recognize that they have the freedom to answer.

A relationship built on grace does not mean two people will always agree. It means they can disagree without turning every difference into a personal injury. It means they can ask without shame, answer without resentment, and respect one another without keeping score.

The next time someone asks you for something, consider whether your answer is honest and whether your response is kind. The next time you need to say no, remember that you can maintain your boundaries without withdrawing your compassion. And the next time someone tells you no, remember that their answer does not automatically mean they love you any less.
